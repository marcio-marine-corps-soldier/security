# The Evolution of Cognitive Warfare in Great Power Competition

## ABSTRACT

Cognitive warfare represents a fundamental shift in how state and non-state actors contest strategic advantage in the international system. Unlike traditional kinetic operations, cognitive warfare targets the psychological, informational, and decision-making processes of adversary populations and institutions through coordinated information operations, psychological influence, and narrative manipulation. This article examines the evolution of cognitive warfare as a central mechanism within great power competition between the United States, China, and Russia. Through analysis of operational precedents, doctrinal development, and emerging technological capabilities, this research identifies cognitive warfare as an asymmetric instrument particularly suited to contexts where direct military confrontation remains constrained by nuclear deterrence and alliance structures. The analysis demonstrates that cognitive warfare has progressed from experimental information operations conducted during the 2016 U.S. election to increasingly sophisticated hybrid campaigns integrating artificial intelligence, deepfakes, and cross-platform coordination. The strategic implications are substantial: cognitive warfare reduces operational costs, achieves plausible deniability, and produces effects that exceed the destruction inherent in traditional warfare. This article presents a risk assessment framework, evaluates future scenarios through 2035, and offers policy recommendations for democratic societies seeking to defend cognitive resilience while developing symmetric and asymmetric cognitive capabilities. The most critical finding indicates that without substantial institutional reform, institutional coordination, and technological innovation, Western nations risk strategic surprise from well-resourced cognitive operations that undermine social cohesion, democratic institutions, and strategic decision-making at decisive moments in future crises.

## INTRODUCTION

The international security environment has undergone a fundamental transformation. The Cold War paradigm of direct superpower competition yielded to a post-9/11 order preoccupied with counterterrorism and counterinsurgency. Beginning approximately 2015, however, the United States, its allies, and strategic competitors have witnessed the emergence of a new competitive phase characterized by simultaneous competition below the threshold of armed conflict, alongside maintenance of latent military capacity. Within this competitive environment, state actors—particularly China and Russia—have invested substantially in capabilities designed not to destroy adversary military forces, but rather to subvert adversary societies, corrupt decision-making processes, and undermine the institutional foundations upon which strategic competitors rely.

Cognitive warfare occupies the apex of this competitive innovation. It is neither propaganda in the traditional sense nor psychological warfare as conceived during the Cold War. Rather, cognitive warfare represents the weaponization of information, narratives, and psychological mechanisms to alter the beliefs, preferences, and decision-making calculus of target populations and leaders. It operates through infiltration of information environments, exploitation of cognitive biases, manipulation of social and institutional trust, and creation of persistent doubt regarding the legitimacy and competence of established authorities.

The evolution of cognitive warfare reflects technological advancement, institutional learning, and strategic adaptation. The 2016 U.S. presidential election demonstrated, at significant but limited scale, how foreign actors could manipulate American information environments to influence electoral outcomes. Subsequent operations—including COVID-19 origin narratives, vaccine hesitancy campaigns, and the weaponization of social movements—revealed escalating sophistication and resource commitment. By 2024-2025, cognitive operations have integrated artificial intelligence, autonomous content generation, personalized targeting, and cross-platform coordination at scales previously impossible. The strategic competitors have developed explicit doctrines, established dedicated organizational structures, and allocated budgets that rival traditional military investments in some categories of operation.

Yet understanding of cognitive warfare remains fragmented within Western policy and intelligence communities. The concept lacks standardized terminology, operational parameters remain unclear, attribution methodologies are insufficiently developed, and defensive measures remain ad hoc rather than systematic. This intellectual and institutional lag creates significant strategic vulnerability precisely at a moment when cognitive operations represent the most likely vector for strategic competition below the threshold of armed conflict.

## RESEARCH QUESTION

How has cognitive warfare evolved as a tool of great power competition, what structural vulnerabilities does it exploit within democratic societies, and what institutional and policy innovations are necessary to establish cognitive resilience sufficient to sustain strategic competition without capitulation or societal fragmentation?

## METHODOLOGY

This research employs a comparative strategic analysis framework integrating qualitative assessment of operational precedents, doctrinal analysis of competitor strategies, and scenario development for future competition. The study draws upon open-source intelligence, published doctrine from competitor nations, academic literature in cognitive psychology and information operations, and unclassified intelligence assessments. The analytical framework incorporates the DIME-FIL model for comprehensive actor assessment, PMESII-PT analysis for environmental evaluation, and ASCOPE methodology for targeting analysis. Risk assessment employs a structured matrix methodology evaluating probability, impact, and strategic consequence. Scenario development utilizes the standard most-likely, high-impact, worst-case, and black swan framework applied across two temporal horizons: 2026-2030 and 2030-2035.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

The academic literature on cognitive warfare remains nascent but rapidly expanding. Early conceptual work emerged from information operations theory developed during the 1990s and 2000s, particularly through the work of theorists examining network-centric warfare and information dominance. The Russian concept of "reflexive control," formalized by Vladimir Lefebvre and subsequently integrated into Russian military doctrine, provides theoretical grounding for understanding how adversaries manipulate perception to achieve strategic objectives.

Contemporary work by scholars including Dmitry Adamsky, Dmitry Adamsky's "The Culture of Military Innovation," and subsequent research on hybrid warfare establishes the conceptual architecture for understanding cognitive operations as distinct from traditional information warfare. The National Defense University's work on cognitive domains, the NATO Strategic Communications Center of Excellence publications, and emerging research from the Irregular Warfare Center provide operational frameworks for assessment.

Intelligence community analysis, particularly from the Defense Intelligence Agency and National Intelligence Council, has progressively refined understanding of competitor cognitive operations. European academic literature, particularly from Scandinavian researchers examining Russian information operations in the Baltic states, provides empirical case studies of operational implementation. Recent work on cognitive resilience, institutional trust, and democratic vulnerability by researchers at Stanford Internet Observatory, MIT Media Lab, and various European research institutions has advanced understanding of defensive mechanisms and societal factors that amplify cognitive warfare effects.

## STRATEGIC CONTEXT

Great power competition has re-emerged as the organizing principle of U.S. national security strategy following approximately two decades of counterterrorism focus. China and Russia, viewing American predominance as temporarily dominant but ultimately reversible, have pursued asymmetric strategies designed to impose costs on American interests, constrain American strategic options, and ultimately displace American influence in regions of contested interest. Direct military competition remains constrained by nuclear deterrence, alliance structures that amplify American capabilities, and Chinese and Russian recognition that military defeat to American forces remains likely in most scenarios.

This competitive environment creates strategic incentives for asymmetric operations that achieve strategic effect below the threshold of conventional military response. Cognitive warfare possesses characteristics that make it optimally suited to this competitive context: it can be conducted at significantly lower cost than conventional military operations; it achieves plausible deniability that complicates attribution and response calculus; it produces strategic effects that exceed the destruction inherent in military operations; and it exploits vulnerabilities specific to open, democratic societies that competitors view as structural weaknesses rather than strengths.

The strategic competitors have recognized that Western information environments, characterized by technological decentralization, minimal gatekeeping, sophisticated advertising and targeting infrastructure, and deep societal polarization, represent critical vulnerabilities. Democratic societies' dependence on public trust, institutional legitimacy, and shared factual understanding provides vectors for exploitation that are unavailable in more controlled information environments. Competitors have progressively weaponized this asymmetry.

## MAIN ANALYSIS

### Conceptual Evolution and Definitional Framework

Cognitive warfare can be defined as coordinated operations conducted across diplomatic, informational, military, economic, financial, and intelligence domains designed to manipulate the decision-making calculus of target populations and leaders through exploitation of cognitive vulnerabilities, degradation of institutional trust, and creation of persistent ambiguity regarding factual reality. This definition distinguishes cognitive warfare from propaganda (which is persuasive but not necessarily deceptive), psychological warfare (which is psychological but conducted primarily through traditional military mechanisms), and standard disinformation (which may be incidental rather than strategically coordinated).

The evolution of cognitive warfare proceeds through distinct operational generations. First-generation operations, exemplified by Cold War-era propaganda, employed centralized, top-down messaging designed to communicate state narratives. These operations were generally attributable, sustained substantial discounting by sophisticated audiences, and required significant organizational capacity to implement at scale. Second-generation operations, developing from 1990-2015, integrated emerging communication technologies, particularly the internet and early social media platforms, to achieve greater reach and targeting precision. The 2016 U.S. election interference campaign represented the first operationally significant second-generation cognitive operation. Russian operations during this period employed troll factories, coordinated social media accounts, and amplification of divisive content designed to heighten polarization and undermine confidence in democratic processes.

Third-generation cognitive operations, emerging from approximately 2020 onward and continuing through the present, integrate artificial intelligence, machine learning, personalized content generation, and cross-platform coordination into unified campaigns. These operations employ deepfakes and synthetic media, utilize behavioral psychology and micro-targeting to achieve precision effects, and operate with sufficient sophistication that attribution becomes substantially difficult and societal response mechanisms prove inadequate. The transition to third-generation operations represents a qualitative leap in capability and strategic impact.

### Competitor Doctrinal Development

China, Russia, and to lesser extent other revisionist actors have developed explicit doctrines governing cognitive operations. Russia's concept of "reflexive control," formalized in military doctrine by the 1990s, articulates explicitly how adversary perception can be manipulated through provision of specially prepared information designed to stimulate predetermined responses. Russian military doctrine, revised following experiences in Georgia, Ukraine, and Syria, integrates information operations as a core component of hybrid warfare, positioned as equally or more important than military operations in achieving strategic objectives.

Chinese doctrine, less explicitly published but evident through operational practice and strategic documents, conceptualizes information warfare as a central component of "Three Warfare" strategy (propaganda warfare, psychological warfare, and public opinion warfare). Chinese strategists view information dominance as prerequisite for military victory and have invested substantially in capabilities for information environment control, both domestically and in contested regions. The concept of "cognitive domain" operations, while not explicitly termed as such in published doctrine, is evident in Chinese strategic writings addressing "information control," "narrative dominance," and "cognitive security."

Russian and Chinese actors have established formal organizational structures dedicated to cognitive operations. Russia maintains the Internet Research Agency and other affiliated organizations dedicated to content generation, platform manipulation, and coordinated amplification. China maintains similar capabilities embedded within state broadcasting organizations and the Ministry of Public Security. Both actors have recruited significant talent in psychology, behavioral science, and data analytics, indicating recognition of cognitive operations as sophisticated technical and strategic discipline.

### Operational Implementation and Case Studies

The operational evolution of cognitive warfare is evident through examination of specific campaigns. The 2016 U.S. election interference, conducted primarily by Russian actors, employed a multi-platform strategy including content creation and amplification on Facebook and Instagram, exploitation of Twitter's retweet mechanisms, infiltration of political discourse on Reddit and other forums, and targeted information leaking designed to influence media coverage and campaign dynamics. The operation achieved effects substantially disproportionate to investment: Russian Internet Research Agency expenditures on social media advertising totaled approximately $100,000, yet generated millions of impressions and demonstrable influence on electoral discourse and voter behavior.

The COVID-19 pandemic generated a wave of sophisticated cognitive operations including origin narrative manipulation, vaccine hesitancy campaigns, and exploitation of health policy disputes to undermine institutional authority. Chinese and Russian actors conducted coordinated campaigns promoting narratives of American responsibility for pandemic origins, amplified anti-vaccine content to undermine public health countermeasures, and exploited legitimate policy disagreements regarding vaccination mandates and pandemic response measures. These operations generated measurable effects including vaccine hesitancy rates exceeding public health requirements for herd immunity in numerous Western communities, political polarization regarding pandemic response, and erosion of public trust in health institutions.

The 2022 invasion of Ukraine generated extensive cognitive operations conducted by both Russian and Ukrainian actors. Russian operations sought to demoralizes Ukrainian resistance, sow doubt regarding international support, and exploit war-weariness in Western societies. Ukrainian operations sought to sustain domestic morale, maintain international support, and delegitimize Russian operational narratives. The Ukraine campaign demonstrated unprecedented scale and sophistication of cognitive operations, with daily content generation at millions of pieces, real-time narrative adaptation responsive to operational developments, and weaponization of social media platforms as primary battlegrounds.

### Technological Enablement and Artificial Intelligence

The integration of artificial intelligence represents a watershed moment in cognitive warfare evolution. Machine learning algorithms enable creation of synthetic content at scale previously impossible through human effort alone. Generative AI models can produce text, images, and video content tailored to specific audiences, cultures, and psychological profiles. Deepfake technology enables creation of synthetic video of political leaders making statements they never made, with video quality increasingly difficult to distinguish from authentic recording.

Large language models, while developed primarily for civilian purposes, possess substantial utility for cognitive warfare. These models can generate persuasive narratives optimized for specific audiences, adapt messaging in real-time to exploit emerging events, generate content in multiple languages simultaneously, and identify psychological vulnerabilities in target populations. The accessibility of these tools—including open-source models available for free download—creates proliferation pathways wherein non-state actors, smaller states, and even well-resourced individuals can conduct sophisticated cognitive operations previously available only to large intelligence and military organizations.

Algorithmic amplification mechanisms inherent to social media platforms multiply the effectiveness of cognitive operations. Platform algorithms, designed to maximize user engagement and time-on-platform, preferentially amplify emotionally evocative content including outrage, divisive narratives, and provocative claims. Cognitive operations exploit this dynamic by generating content designed to trigger algorithmic amplification, achieving reach and impact amplified orders of magnitude beyond organic distribution. The combination of AI-generated content and algorithmic amplification creates feedback loop mechanisms wherein cognitive operations can achieve effects approaching information dominance within targeted geographic or demographic segments.

### Exploitation of Societal Vulnerabilities

The efficacy of cognitive warfare against democratic societies reflects exploitation of structural vulnerabilities specific to open information environments. First, democratic societies require public trust in institutions, shared factual understanding, and acceptance of legitimate authority as prerequisites for institutional function. Cognitive operations systematically target each of these requirements, creating ambiguity regarding facts, undermining confidence in institutions, and delegitimizing established authority structures. As trust declines and factual agreement erodes, institutional capacity to respond to crises deteriorates.

Second, the political economy of attention in digital information environments creates conditions favorable to sensationalism, polarization, and outrage rather than nuanced understanding. Cognitive operations exploit this dynamic by creating false narratives specifically designed to achieve amplification. The cost of generating attention through authentic expertise or careful analysis vastly exceeds the cost of generating attention through false claims and outrage. This dynamic tilts competitive advantage toward actors willing to abandon factual accuracy in favor of narrative impact.

Third, individual psychological vulnerabilities including cognitive biases, susceptibility to social proof, and preference for information confirming pre-existing beliefs create conditions wherein cognitive operations can achieve effects at scale. Behavioral psychology research demonstrates that individuals preferentially consume information confirming existing beliefs, exhibit confirmation bias that strengthens rather than challenges pre-existing views when exposed to contradictory information, and are substantially influenced by social proof mechanisms wherein perceived agreement of reference groups shapes individual beliefs. Cognitive operations exploit these vulnerabilities systematically.

Fourth, geographic and demographic polarization within Western societies creates conditions wherein targeted cognitive operations can generate dramatic effects through relatively modest resource investment. Communities deeply divided by geography, values, and demographic characteristics share progressively divergent informational diets, consume different media sources, and operate within distinct epistemic communities. Cognitive operations targeting specific geographic or demographic communities can achieve amplification and effects disproportionate to resources invested.

## INTELLIGENCE ASSESSMENT

### Key Judgments

1. Russia and China are conducting sustained, sophisticated cognitive operations against the United States and allied nations with resource commitment, doctrinal development, and operational sophistication increasing annually.

2. Artificial intelligence integration represents a watershed advancement in cognitive warfare capability, enabling content generation, targeting, and narrative adaptation at scales previously impossible.

3. Democratic societies lack coherent cognitive defense strategies, inter-agency coordination mechanisms, and institutional capacity to detect, attribute, and respond to cognitive operations at operationally relevant timescales.

4. Cognitive operations targeting electoral processes represent the highest-priority threat due to cascading consequences for institutional legitimacy and strategic decision-making.

5. The private technology sector possesses greater capability and resources than government entities to address cognitive threats, yet operates under financial incentives that frequently conflict with security objectives.

### Confidence Levels

- **High Confidence**: Russian and Chinese states are conducting cognitive operations; these operations are increasing in sophistication and scale.
- **Moderate Confidence**: Specific attribution of operational origin; assessment of operational effects on specific policy outcomes.
- **Low Confidence**: Prediction of future operational targets; assessment of relative effectiveness of defensive measures; economic costs of cognitive operations.

### Indicators and Warnings

- Sudden spikes in coordinated social media activity preceding significant political events
- Appearance of sophisticated deepfake or synthetic media content
- Narratives exhibiting linguistic patterns inconsistent with organic origin
- Cascading media amplification following initial small-scale platform seeding
- Narrative content addressing fractious domestic political disputes with sudden amplification
- Cross-platform coordination suggesting centralized direction rather than organic emergence

### Collection Gaps

- Quantitative assessment of cognitive operation effectiveness on specific policy outcomes
- Detailed understanding of decision-making processes within Chinese cognitive warfare organizations
- Economic cost assessments for conducting large-scale third-generation cognitive operations
- Understanding of competitor risk tolerance for attribution and escalation
- Effectiveness assessment of defensive countermeasures and cognitive hardening initiatives

### Intelligence Requirements

1. Real-time detection and characterization of emerging cognitive operations
2. Attribution mechanisms providing decision-maker confidence for response determination
3. Assessment of competitor intentions and thresholds for cognitive operation escalation
4. Understanding of cognitive operation effects on specific target populations and demographics
5. Evaluation of defensive measure effectiveness and optimization

### Most Likely Scenario (2026-2030)

Sustained escalation of sophisticated cognitive operations targeting electoral processes in the United States and allied democracies. Operations increasingly integrate artificial intelligence for content generation, achieving unprecedented targeting precision and narrative adaptation. Operations achieve measurable effects on electoral behavior, though insufficient to determine electoral outcomes without other factors. Cognitive operations targeting policy areas including healthcare, energy, and defense generate increased institutional skepticism and policy paralysis. International coordination of defensive mechanisms remains insufficient to address threat magnitude. By 2030, cognitive operations represent the primary vector for great power competition below armed conflict threshold.

### Most Dangerous Scenario (2026-2035)

Coordinated cognitive operations from multiple state actors targeting critical decision-making by military, political, and economic leaders during period of acute international crisis. Operations exploit uncertainty inherent in crisis decision-making to manipulate perceptions of threat severity, adversary intent, and alliance reliability. Cognitive operations generate false indicators suggesting imminent attack, generating pressure for preemptive military action. Degraded institutional trust and information environment corruption limit decision-makers' ability to verify information, distinguish authentic intelligence from deception, or achieve organizational consensus. Decision-making becomes subject to information warfare effects rather than strategic calculation. Strategic miscalculation and unintended escalation result in military conflict between major powers at substantially elevated intensity and risk of nuclear weapon employment.

## FUTURE SCENARIOS (2026–2035)

### Scenario 1: Managed Competition

Democratic societies develop coherent cognitive defense strategies integrating institutional reform, technological innovation, and educational initiatives. Technology platforms implement substantial modifications to algorithmic amplification and content moderation. International coordination mechanisms for attribution and response establish deterrence against cognitive operations. Cognitive operations continue at elevated levels but within bounds manageable by institutional defensive mechanisms. Strategic competition continues primarily through cognitive and economic channels rather than military escalation. This scenario reflects moderate optimism regarding institutional capacity for adaptation.

### Scenario 2: Escalation Through Innovation

Artificial intelligence advancement accelerates beyond current projections. Synthetic media becomes indistinguishable from authentic content even to trained observers. Autonomous cognitive operations conduct real-time narrative adaptation responsive to competitor actions. International coordination of defensive mechanisms fails due to disagreement regarding appropriate response levels and institutional capacity limitations. Cognitive operations escalate in sophistication and scale, generating measurable effects on electoral outcomes, policy decisions, and institutional trust. Strategic competition increasingly occurs through cognitive channels, with military competition remaining constrained but risks of miscalculation increasing due to information warfare effects.

### Scenario 3: Institutional Failure

Democratic societies prove insufficiently adaptive. Institutional defensive mechanisms remain inadequate to threat magnitude. Technology platforms resist meaningful reform due to economic incentives favoring engagement amplification over security. International coordination mechanisms fail to develop. Cognitive operations achieve effects sufficient to generate electoral dispute, policy paralysis, and institutional erosion. Public trust in democratic institutions declines below sustainability threshold. Political legitimacy erodes. Strategic decision-making becomes subject to information warfare effects. Domestic political conflicts intensify as competing narratives regarding basic factual reality make democratic discourse impossible. Democratic institutions weaken, potentially enabling authoritarian transformation.

### Scenario 4: Black Swan—Cognitive Operation During Crisis

Acute international crisis emerges from developments in Taiwan Strait, Middle East, or Eastern Europe. Competitors conduct sophisticated cognitive operations targeting decision-makers and public opinion. Operations generate false indicators of threat, misrepresent military capabilities, or manipulate perception of alliance reliability. Degraded information environment prevents verification of information, distinguishes authentic intelligence from deception operations, or achieves organizational consensus. Decision-making occurs under conditions of substantial information corruption. Military action results from cognitive warfare effects rather than strategic calculation. Conflict escalates beyond predicted parameters due to information-induced miscalculation.

## POLICY IMPLICATIONS

The strategic implications of cognitive warfare require fundamental institutional adaptation. First, democratic societies must recognize cognitive warfare as existential threat distinct from traditional national security challenges, warranting resource commitment and institutional priority equivalent to conventional military threats. Second, cognitive defense requires integration across government, private sector, and civil society in manner substantially exceeding current institutional arrangements. Third, cognitive warfare represents challenge to democratic institutions specifically, requiring defensive measures that preserve democratic values while establishing resilience against cognitive operations.

The current institutional approach, characterized by fragmented responsibility, insufficient resource allocation, limited inter-agency coordination, and minimal engagement with technology platforms, is inadequate. Defensive mechanisms must address information environment architecture, institutional trust and legitimacy, individual cognitive resilience, and decision-making processes for military and political leaders. Technological solutions alone are insufficient; institutional and educational innovation is equally critical.

## RECOMMENDATIONS

### Institutional Reforms

1. **Establishment of Unified Cognitive Defense Command**: Create dedicated organizational structure with authority for integrating federal, state, local, and private sector defensive efforts. This command should possess authority for strategic communications, intelligence assessment, platform engagement, and rapid response to emerging operations.

2. **Intelligence Community Reorganization**: Establish dedicated intelligence collection and analysis elements focused exclusively on competitor cognitive operations. Current intelligence capacity is distributed across multiple agencies without adequate integration or specialization.

3. **Cross-Agency Coordination Mechanisms**: Establish formal mechanisms for inter-agency coordination during cognitive operations. Current processes lack authority, clarity, and integration necessary for rapid response.

### Technology Sector Engagement

1. **Platform Transparency Requirements**: Mandate comprehensive disclosure of algorithmic amplification mechanisms, content moderation decisions, and platform-level engagement metrics. Current opacity prevents understanding of cognitive operation amplification dynamics.

2. **Algorithmic Modification Requirements**: Require platform modifications to reduce preferential amplification of divisive or sensationalist content. Current algorithms create conditions favorable to cognitive operations.

3. **Coordinated Threat Intelligence Sharing**: Establish mechanisms for technology platforms to share threat intelligence regarding emerging operations while maintaining appropriate privacy protections.

### Educational and Cultural Initiatives

1. **Cognitive Resilience Curriculum**: Integrate cognitive bias awareness, psychological influence recognition, and information evaluation into educational curricula from secondary school through university.

2. **Institutional Trust Restoration**: Develop initiatives for rebuilding public trust in institutions through transparency, demonstrated competence, and communication demonstrating institutional responsiveness to public concerns.

3. **Narrative Counter-Capability**: Develop capacity for rapid generation and amplification of counter-narratives to competitor cognitive operations.

### International Coordination

1. **Allied Intelligence Sharing**: Establish mechanisms for sharing intelligence regarding cognitive operations with allied nations, enabling coordinated response.

2. **Attribution Frameworks**: Develop standardized attribution methodologies enabling consensus regarding operational origin, facilitating coordinated response.

3. **Deterrence Mechanisms**: Develop mechanisms for communicating to competitors the costs of cognitive operations, potentially including sanctions, intelligence operations, or other responses.

## CONCLUSION

Cognitive warfare represents a fundamental innovation in strategic competition, exploiting vulnerabilities specific to democratic societies while remaining below the threshold of military response. The evolution from first-generation propaganda through third-generation artificial intelligence-enabled operations has proceeded with remarkable speed, with strategic competitors investing substantially in capability development while democratic societies have responded with insufficient speed and resource commitment.

The strategic implications are substantial. Cognitive warfare enables achievement of strategic objectives at costs substantially lower than conventional military operations, with reduced risk of escalation and greater plausibility of deniability. As artificial intelligence capabilities advance, the sophistication, scale, and effectiveness of cognitive operations will continue to increase. Without substantial institutional adaptation, democratic societies risk strategic surprise from well-resourced cognitive operations that undermine institutional legitimacy, fracture social cohesion, and degrade decision-making capacity precisely when strategic clarity is most essential.

The recommendations offered in this analysis require political will, substantial resource commitment, and institutional innovation. Yet the alternative—passivity and inadequate response—virtually ensures that cognitive warfare will become the primary vector for great power competition, with consequences extending far beyond electoral influence to fundamental questions of institutional survival and democratic governance.

The 2026-2035 period will likely determine whether democratic societies successfully develop adequate cognitive defense while maintaining democratic values, or whether inadequate institutional response enables progressive erosion of democratic institutions through information warfare mechanisms. This assessment represents not alarmism but rather realistic evaluation of trajectory and threat magnitude. Strategic competitors have demonstrated willingness to invest substantially in cognitive warfare capability. Without equivalent commitment to cognitive defense, the strategic balance will tilt progressively toward actors willing to abandon factual accuracy and institutional legitimacy in pursuit of strategic advantage.
